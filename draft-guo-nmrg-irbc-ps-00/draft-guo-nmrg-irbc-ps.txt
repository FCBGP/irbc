    Inter-Domain Routing Behavior Commitments: Problem Statement and
                              Requirements
                     draft-guo-nmrg-irbc-ps-latest

Abstract

   This document examines whether a routing-specific commitment model is
   useful for expressing and assessing an AS's own expected BGP behavior
   across administrative-domain boundaries.  An Inter-Domain Routing
   Behavior Commitment (IRBC) is a candidate declaration.  It is
   attributable to an issuer and applies to specific inter-domain
   connections, route scopes, directions, and operating conditions.  Its
   semantics include the subject AS's connection role, the associated
   routing behavior, and a commitment context that defines scope,
   conditions, exceptions, and validity.

   The document distinguishes an IRBC from a general IBN intent, an
   interconnection service request, local routing policy, route-path
   authorization, and cryptographic evidence of an individual route-
   propagation event.

1.  Introduction

   The Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) defined in [RFC4271] enables
   independently administered Autonomous Systems (ASes) to exchange
   reachability information.  Each AS determines its routing behavior
   through local policies, operational procedures, topology, and
   configuration.  An AS may have expectations about how it will
   originate, accept, select, advertise, or propagate routes for a
   particular inter-domain connection.  An AS operator may make
   statements about its routing behavior.

   These expectations and behaviors can be typically configured using
   existing routing-policy mechanisms.  The issue addressed here is not
   the absence of mechanisms to configure or signal routing behavior.
   It is whether a commonly understood, issuer-attributable commitment
   model adds value by bringing together the identity and authority of
   the issuer, the AS behavior being declared, the applicable scope and
   conditions, and the evidence available to an external assessor.

   Intent-Based Networking (IBN) provides general concepts for
   expressing network goals at an appropriate level of abstraction and
   translating those goals into network actions.  IBN also considers
   monitoring and assurance to assess whether observed network behavior
   remains consistent with an intent refering to [RFC9315].  Intent
   classification and scope are further discussed in [RFC9316].  These
   concepts provide the general foundation for this document.
   [I-D.irtf-nmrg-ibn-usecases] has considered IBN for routing and path
   selection, IBN for interconnection, and multi-domain intent
   resolution.  [I-D.contreras-nmrg-interconnection-intents] has
   considered conventional IP peering and more advanced interconnection
   services.

   However, this document examines a more specific question: How can an
   AS make an attributable declaration about its own expected BGP
   routing behavior, scoped to defined inter-domain connections and
   route classes, so that another independently administered domain can
   understand the declaration and assess it against suitable routing
   evidence?

   A general network intent and a routing behavior commitment may serve
   different purposes.  An intent typically expresses operational goals
   and desired outcomes for a network or service.  A routing behavior
   commitment is a more explicit statement attributable to a particular
   AS or an entity authorized to act on its behalf, describing behavior
   for which that AS takes operational responsibility.

   This distinction matters in inter-domain environments.  The AS that
   makes a declaration, the AS that consumes it, and the entity that
   observes routing behavior may be in different administrative domains.
   A remote observer may not have access to the issuer's internal policy
   configuration, complete routing information bases, or all Adj-RIB-Out
   state.  Consequently, a declaration needs to identify the behavior
   being claimed and its scope, while an assessment needs to state what
   the available evidence can and cannot establish.

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",

   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all

2.1.  Inter-Domain Routing Behavior Commitment

   An Inter-Domain Routing Behavior Commitment (IRBC) is a candidate,
   issuer-attributable declaration in which an AS or an entity
   authorized to act on its behalf states the routing behavior that the
   subject AS undertakes to provide or enforce for specified inter-
   domain connections, route scopes, directions, and operating
   conditions.  An IRBC may identify: (1) the subject's connection role
   in the specified inter-domain relationship; (2) the associated
   routing behavior; and (3) the commitment context that defines
   applicable scope, direction, conditions, exceptions, validity, and
   responsibility.

   The term commitment denotes an attributable operational statement of
   responsibility.  It does not by itself establish that the behavior
   has been followed, create a legal obligation, or establish a
   bilateral agreement.  The need for a distinct IRBC model, rather than
   an extension or specialization of an existing interconnection-intent
   representation, is a question for further.

2.2.  Issuer and Subject

   The issuer is the entity that creates and publishes or otherwise
   provides an IRBC.  The subject is the AS or routing domain whose
   behavior is described.  The issuer may be the subject itself or an
   entity authorized to act on its behalf.  A future model needs to
   distinguish authority over an AS number, authority over address
   resources, and operational authority over routing policy where those
   authorities differ.

2.3.  Connection Role

   A connection role describes the functional relationship or
   responsibility of the subject AS with respect to a specified
   neighboring AS or inter-domain connection.  A role is relative to the
   connection and may be scoped by route class or operational condition.
   It is not necessarily a global property of an AS and is not limited
   to the finite BGP Role values defined in [RFC9234].

2.4.  Declared Behavior

   Declared behavior is the routing behavior specified by an IRBC.  A
   declaration may express a required behavior, a prohibited behavior, a
   permitted behavior, or a preference.  These categories have different
   semantics and MUST NOT be treated as interchangeable.  The connection
   role provides context for the behavior but does not, by itself,
   determine all behavior semantics.

2.5.  Commitment Context

   The commitment context describes the conditions under which declared
   behavior applies.  It may include route scope, neighboring AS,
   direction, AFI/SAFI, time interval, operational state, exceptions,
   and precedence among applicable statements.  It does not imply a
   legal contract or a reciprocal commitment by another AS.

2.6.  Observed Behavior

   Observed behavior is information about routing activity or state
   obtained at a particular observation point and time.  Examples
   include BGP UPDATE messages, Adj-RIB-In or Adj-RIB-Out information,
   RIB data, BMP feeds, and other operational evidence.

2.7.  Evidence

   Evidence is information used to support or refute a claim about
   observed routing behavior.  Evidence has provenance, collection time,
   observation scope, coverage, and limitations.  The absence of an
   observation is not necessarily evidence that an event did not occur.

2.8.  External Assessment

   External assessment is an evaluation of an IRBC by an entity that is
   independent of, or outside the administrative control of, the issuer.
   An assessment may report that available evidence supports compliance,
   supports a violation, or is insufficient to reach either conclusion.

3.  Inter-Domain Routing Scenarios

3.1.  Route-Class-Specific Export Commitment

   AS A has an eBGP connection with AS B and applies different export
   behavior to different route classes.  For example, customer routes
   may be exported to B, while routes learned from another provider are
   not exported to B, except under a defined operational condition.  AS
   A can implement this behavior using local import and export policy.
   Such a policy may also be configured and managed using existing
   policy models.

   An interconnection intent may specify the desired routing service or
   associated requirements.  The IRBC question is whether AS A can
   additionally provide an attributable declaration that identifies the
   route classes, neighbor, direction, exceptions, and conditions for
   which AS A takes responsibility.  An external assessor would need
   evidence from appropriate observation points to evaluate observed
   advertisements.  A collector that does not observe an advertisement
   cannot, by that fact alone, prove that AS A did not advertise the
   route to another neighbor.

3.2.  Conditional Backup Advertisement

   AS A declares that it will advertise a specified route class to
   backup neighbor AS C only when a defined primary-path condition is
   met.  The commitment MUST distinguish a mandatory condition from a
   preference and identify the time interval and routing scope to which
   it applies.

   An external assessment requires evidence about both the advertisement
   to AS C and the condition claimed to trigger it.  A BGP UPDATE
   observed at one collector may establish that an advertisement
   occurred at that observation point.  It may not establish the
   complete state of AS A's internal route selection or prove that the
   primary path was unavailable.  The assessment model MUST state when
   the evidence is sufficient and when the result is indeterminate.

3.3.  Scope-Dependent Interconnection Behavior

   Two ASes may apply different routing arrangements to different route
   classes over what is operationally viewed as the same
   interconnection.  [RFC9234] calls a peering relationship "Complex"
   when more than one BGP Role applies between the same ASes, for
   example when the relationship is Provider-to-Customer for some
   prefixes and Peer-to-Peer for others.  RFC 9234 specifies that BGP
   Roles are not configured on an eBGP session with a Complex peering
   relationship.  It is worth noting that per-prefix policies may be
   used, but there are no in-band measures in that specification to
   check the correctness of the per-prefix peering configuration.

   This scenario may motivate a route-scope-specific declaration, but it
   does not establish that IRBC is necessary.  The MUST identify exactly
   what an IRBC would add beyond the per-prefix policy, existing role
   and OTC behavior, and the available routing evidence.  It MUST also
   consider whether the same requirement can be met by multiple eBGP
   sessions, RPA objects, or an extension of an existing
   interconnection-intent representation.

3.4.  Control-Plane and Data-Plane Boundary

   An operator may state an expectation that traffic should traverse a
   preferred provider or path.  However, an AS_PATH observed in the BGP
   control plane does not necessarily establish the actual data-plane
   forwarding path.  Internal routing, tunnels, policy-based forwarding,
   ECMP, and other mechanisms may affect packet forwarding.

   The initial IRBC scope therefore focuses on BGP control-plane
   behavior, such as route origination, advertisement, propagation, and
   selected attribute handling.  Data-plane commitments are outside the
   initial scope unless the required data-plane evidence and observation
   model are separately defined.

4.  Problem Statement

4.1.  From Local Policy to an Attributable External Declaration

   An AS can implement routing behavior using local policy, but that
   configuration does not automatically provide another domain with an
   interoperable statement of the behavior the AS claims it will
   perform.  Conversely, a published statement does not demonstrate that
   the AS follows it.  A model may be useful to distinguish internal
   policy, an externally understandable commitment, and evidence of
   actual behavior.  The need for a distinct model MUST be evaluated
   against existing interconnection-intent structures and IBN assurance
   functions.

4.2.  Issuer, Subject, and Authority

   A declaration can be assessed only if the assessor can identify the
   issuer, the AS or routing domain that is the subject, and the
   authority under which the declaration was made.  The relevant
   authority may concern operation of the AS, address resources, or
   control of routing policy.  These do not necessarily belong to the
   same entity.  A general model MUST NOT assume that possession of an
   ASN or a prefix resource automatically proves authority to make every
   routing-policy commitment for an AS.

4.3.  Scope and Behavioral Semantics

   A statement such as "do not export this route" is incomplete without
   identifying the route, neighbor or neighbor class, address family,
   direction, time interval, and applicable conditions.  A commitment
   may express a mandatory behavior, a prohibition, a permission, or a
   preference.  These meanings are distinguishable.  The model MUST also
   address overlapping scopes, exceptions, and conflicting statements.

4.4.  Evidence and External Observability

   A commitment does not create the evidence required to assess it.  A
   remote observer may have access to public BGP data, selected route
   collectors, a limited set of RIBs, BMP feeds, or evidence voluntarily
   supplied by the issuer.  These sources have different coverage and
   trust properties.  BMP defined in [RFC7854] provides a protocol for
   obtaining route views from BGP speakers.  Support for Adj-RIB-Out
   monitoring is specified in [RFC8671].  Neither a monitoring feed nor
   a public collector view SHOULD be assumed to provide complete
   evidence for every neighbor and every route.

   The model MUST relate each behavior claim to the evidence needed to
   assess it and MUST represent observation points, time intervals,
   provenance, coverage, and limitations.  Evidence that a route was
   observed at one point does not prove that it was or was not
   advertised to every other neighbor.  A missing observation MUST NOT
   automatically be interpreted as proof that an event did not occur.

4.5.  Unilateral and Bilateral Statements

   An AS may make a unilateral declaration about its own behavior.  A
   bilateral interconnection arrangement may require corresponding
   declarations or an explicit agreement by both parties.  A declaration
   from AS A cannot establish consent or a commitment by AS B.  A model
   MUST preserve the identity and authority of each issuer and provide a
   way to represent disagreement or inconsistent declarations without
   silently treating one party's statement as authoritative for the
   other.

4.6.  Assessment and Uncertainty

   The available evidence may support compliance, support a violation,
   or be insufficient to reach either conclusion.  A binary result is
   therefore not always appropriate.  An assessment model SHOULD
   distinguish:

   *  Compliant: the available evidence supports that the declared
      behavior was followed within the stated scope and assessment
      conditions.

   *  Violation: the available evidence supports that the declared
      behavior was not followed within the stated scope and assessment
      conditions.

   *  Indeterminate: the evidence is insufficient, incomplete,
      conflicting, or inapplicable to reach either conclusion.

   These outcomes describe assessment confidence and evidence
   sufficiency.  They do not imply that every routing behavior can be
   independently verified in all deployments.

4.7.  Lifecycle and Information Disclosure

   Routing policies and inter-domain relationships change.  A
   declaration may be updated, superseded, withdrawn, or become
   inapplicable when a connection or route scope changes.  An assessor
   may retain stale or delayed information.  A model MUST address
   validity, versioning, update, withdrawal, revocation, and the
   relationship between an observation interval and the applicable
   declaration version.

   At the same time, publishing a commitment may reveal sensitive
   information about topology, customers, peering, or traffic
   engineering.  The model SHOULD identify the minimum information
   required for a meaningful assessment and the limits imposed by
   selective disclosure.

5.  Positioning and Gap

   The gap is the absence of a cross-domain, attributable, scope-aware,
   evidence-relative framework for expressing and assessing an AS's own
   BGP behavior commitments.  Specifically:

   *  No existing mechanism binds a behavior claim to an issuer,
      subject, and authority in a way that an external domain can
      verify.

   *  No existing mechanism scopes a behavior claim to connection, route
      class, direction, and condition with explicit semantics for
      mandatory, prohibited, permitted, and preferred behaviors.

   *  No existing mechanism defines how external evidence maps to a
      compliance assessment, including the explicit representation of
      indeterminate outcomes.

6.  Requirements

6.1.  Issuer and Subject Attribution

   A model SHOULD identify the issuer, the subject AS or routing domain,
   and the authority under which the issuer makes the declaration.  It
   SHOULD distinguish self-issued declarations from delegated
   declarations and SHOULD NOT infer routing-policy authority solely
   from prefix ownership or ASN registration.

6.2.  Self-Commitment and Party Separation

   A declaration SHOULD be limited to behavior for which the subject or
   issuer has responsibility.  It MUST NOT imply that another AS has
   accepted a reciprocal commitment.  Where a bilateral arrangement is
   relevant, each party's declaration and evidence SHOULD remain
   attributable to that party.

6.3.  Routing-Specific Scope

   A model SHOULD support scope dimensions relevant to inter-domain
   routing, which may include:

   *  subject AS and neighboring AS or inter-domain connection

   *  AFI/SAFI

   *  prefix, prefix set, origin AS, or route class

   *  ingress or egress direction

   *  relevant BGP behavior, such as origination, advertisement,
      propagation, or attribute handling

   *  validity interval and operational conditions

   *  explicit exceptions and exclusions.

   These dimensions are a routing-specific application of the intent
   scope and network scope concepts in [RFC9316], not a replacement for
   the general taxonomy.

6.4.  Behavior Semantics

   A model SHOULD distinguish mandatory behavior, prohibited behavior,
   permitted behavior, and preferred behavior.  It SHOULD define how
   conditional statements, exceptions, and precedence are interpreted.
   A preference MUST NOT be treated as a mandatory obligation, and a
   permission MUST NOT be treated as a requirement to perform an action.

   Connection roles may be included as context for a declaration.  They
   SHOULD NOT be equated with the finite BGP Role values defined in
   [RFC9234], nor SHOULD the model redefine the protocol behavior
   associated with those values.

6.5.  Claim-to-Evidence Relationship

   A model SHOULD identify, for each supported behavior claim, the
   evidence types that may support an assessment and the limits of those
   evidence types.  Evidence metadata SHOULD include provenance,
   observation point, collection time, coverage, and integrity
   properties where available.  The model SHOULD permit an indeterminate
   result when the evidence cannot support a reliable conclusion.

6.6.  External Assessment

   A model SHOULD allow an assessor outside the issuer's administrative
   domain to interpret the declaration and report an assessment without
   assuming access to the issuer's complete internal configuration or
   all routing state.  It SHOULD state when issuer cooperation or
   evidence from internal observation points is necessary.

6.7.  Lifecycle and Consistency

   A model SHOULD address creation, publication, update, supersession,
   withdrawal, validity, and revocation.  It SHOULD enable an assessor
   to identify which declaration version applies to a given observation
   interval and to recognize stale, conflicting, or inconsistent
   declarations.

6.8.  Disclosure and Deployment

   A model SHOULD minimize unnecessary disclosure of internal policy and
   topology while exposing enough information to make the commitment
   meaningful and assessable.  It SHOULD also consider partial
   deployment, missing declarations, unsupported behavior types, and
   unavailable evidence.  Absence of an IRBC MUST NOT be interpreted as
   evidence of misbehavior.

7.  Security Considerations

   An externally assessable routing behavior commitment introduces
   security considerations because declarations may influence how other
   domains interpret an AS's behavior.  This document identifies issues
   for further and does not specify a complete security architecture.

   An attacker may publish a declaration while impersonating an AS
   operator or may publish a declaration for an AS that the issuer does
   not control.  A future mechanism needs to authenticate the issuer and
   determine whether the issuer is authorized to make statements for the
   subject AS and declared scope.  Authority over an ASN, address
   resources, and routing policy may be held by different entities;
   these forms of authority MUST NOT be conflated.

   A declaration may be modified, substituted, replayed, or rolled back
   to an earlier version.  A future model needs to consider integrity
   protection, version identification, validity intervals, freshness,
   update, withdrawal, and revocation.

   An issuer may provide different declarations to different observers,
   intentionally or accidentally.  A signature on an individual
   declaration does not by itself prevent equivocation.  Further work
   may need to consider consistency mechanisms, auditability, or
   transparency services.

   An authentic declaration may describe behavior that the issuer does
   not follow.  Authentication establishes the origin of a declaration,
   not compliance with it.  Assessment requires appropriate evidence and
   MUST account for incomplete observation and the possibility that some
   behavior is not externally observable.

   Evidence may be forged, altered, selectively disclosed, or collected
   from insufficient observation points.  A future framework needs to
   state the trust placed in routers, collectors, monitoring systems,
   and evidence providers.  It MUST NOT treat an absence of data as
   proof that an event did not occur.

   Large numbers of declarations, overlapping scopes, or complex
   conditional statements may increase storage, distribution, and
   assessment costs.  A future design SHOULD consider bounded
   complexity, resource limits, and input validation.

   Declarations may reveal topology, peering relationships, customer
   categories, or traffic-engineering preferences.  A future design
   SHOULD evaluate what information MUST be disclosed to support a
   meaningful assessment and what information can remain internal.

   An assessor may apply a declaration outside its scope or confuse a
   preference with a mandatory behavior.  A declaration by one AS MUST
   NOT be interpreted as proof that another AS has agreed to a
   reciprocal behavior.  Scope, conditions, exceptions, and issuer
   responsibility MUST therefore be explicit.

Appendix A.  Scope and Non-Goals

   This document focuses on the problem statement and requirements for
   issuer-attributable, externally assessable routing behavior
   commitments in inter-domain BGP environments.

   The following topics are outside the scope of this document:

   *  redefining the general concepts, taxonomy, lifecycle, or
      architecture of IBN

   *  defining a general-purpose intent language, schema, ontology, or
      translation engine

   *  defining an IBN controller, multi-domain orchestrator, policy
      compiler, or closed-loop automation architecture

   *  defining a general interconnection-intent model or replacing the
      work in [I-D.contreras-nmrg-interconnection-intents]

   *  defining a complete routing-policy language or replacing RPSL or
      Routing Policy YANG

   *  defining an IRBC wire format, serialization, or information model

   *  defining a new BGP attribute, community, capability, or AFI/SAFI

   *  defining an RPKI signed-object profile, trust-anchor model, or
      distribution protocol

   *  defining a universal verification algorithm or claiming that all
      routing behavior can be externally verified

   *  replacing ROA/ROV, ASPA, ASRA, RPA, BGPsec, FC-BGP, BGP Roles, or
      OTC

   *  defining data-plane forwarding commitments in the absence of a
      corresponding evidence model.

   IBN systems may be used to generate, manage, or assess routing
   behavior commitments.  Existing routing-security mechanisms may
   provide authorization or evidence relevant to an assessment.  Any
   future protocol or information-model work requires separate analysis
   and coordination with the appropriate IETF or IRTF groups.

Appendix B.  Relationship to Existing and Mechanisms

   This section identifies the capabilities that an IRBC model would
   reuse and the areas that require further investigation.  It does not
   imply that the referenced work is deficient or that an IRBC MUST be
   implemented as a new protocol object.

B.1.  IBN

   IRBC is related to IBN, but it is not a replacement for or a new
   version of the general IBN model.  IRBC and IBN operate at different
   levels of abstraction and address different questions.  IBN is a
   management paradigm that translates operator goals into network
   actions and closes the loop through monitoring and assurance.  IRBC
   is a cross-domain declaration and assessment paradigm.  IRBC asks how
   an AS can make an attributable, scoped statement about its own BGP
   behavior, and how an external domain can evaluate that statement
   using limited evidence.  An IBN system MAY consume or produce IRBCs,
   but IRBC does not depend on an IBN system for its definition or
   validity.

   [RFC9315] describes general IBN concepts and functions, including
   intent fulfillment, intent translation, orchestration, monitoring,
   intent compliance assessment, and the intent lifecycle.  [RFC9316]
   provides a methodology for classifying intents, including intent
   users, intent types, intent scope, network scope, abstraction, and
   lifecycle.  This document reuses these foundations and does not
   define another general intent taxonomy or lifecycle.

   An IRBC is a routing-specific declaration associated with an AS's
   responsibility for its own inter-domain routing behavior.  It is more
   explicit and technically scoped than a high-level service goal.  An
   IBN system may derive an IRBC from an operator's intent and local
   policy, exchange it with another domain, and use monitoring or
   evidence services to assess it.  Those IBN system functions are
   outside the definition of an IRBC.

   The distinction from general Intent Assurance is also specific.
   [RFC9315] already describes the comparison of observed network
   behavior with intended behavior.  The question here is how such
   assurance applies when the declaration is attributable to one AS, the
   assessor belongs to another administrative domain, and the evidence
   is limited to what can be obtained or trusted across that boundary.
   This document therefore investigates a routing-specific external-
   assessment problem; it does not claim to introduce the general
   concept of assurance.

   [RFC9315] defines general IBN concepts and functions, including
   intent fulfillment and intent assurance.  In particular, Intent
   Assurance compares observed network behavior with the behavior
   expected by an intent.  [RFC9316] provides dimensions for classifying
   intents, including user, type, scope, network scope, abstraction, and
   lifecycle.  IRBC SHOULD reuse these foundations.  It SHOULD NOT
   introduce a second general definition of intent, a competing intent
   taxonomy, a new generic intent-scope model, or a separate generic
   intent lifecycle.  The routing-specific question under consideration
   is whether a declaration attributable to a particular AS or
   authorized operator, and intended to be assessed by another
   administrative domain, requires additional semantics beyond the
   general IBN concepts.

   [I-D.irtf-nmrg-ibn-usecases] discusses IBN for routing and path
   selection, including service-function chaining and SRv6-oriented
   scenarios.  It also includes an IBN-for-Interconnection scenario in
   which one provider selects an interconnection intent and specifies
   target resources, another provider maps intent types to protocols or
   APIs and establishes or provisions the requested resources, and
   monitoring information is collected and analyzed during the assurance
   phase.  This work already covers routing-related intent,
   interconnection fulfillment, monitoring-based assurance, and multi-
   domain intent considerations.  IRBC MUST NOT be presented as filling
   a general absence of these capabilities.  Its proposed focus is
   narrower: a declaration attributable to the subject AS about that
   AS's own BGP control-plane behavior, together with the evidence
   semantics relevant to an assessor outside the subject's
   administrative domain.

   [I-D.contreras-nmrg-interconnection-intents] considers the use of
   intents to express advanced interconnection requirements beyond
   traditional IP peering.  It discusses interconnection intent types,
   an intent lifecycle, protocol aspects, and a declarative intent
   structure.  The structure includes Expectation, Target, Condition,
   and Context.  Its conventional IP peering example includes peer AS
   identification, peer IP address, peering authentication, and traffic
   levels.  Its fulfillment and assurance procedures describe a provider
   requesting or specifying resources, a provider mapping intent to
   protocols or APIs and provisioning resources, monitoring telemetry,
   internal checks of committed service-level objectives, and reporting
   or analyzing metrics against the request.

   [I-D.irtf-nmrg-ibn-usecases] also refers to the interconnection-
   intent work when describing its interconnection scenario.
   Accordingly, IRBC MUST NOT claim novelty for cross-domain intent
   expression, peer identification, intent targets, conditions, context,
   fulfillment, or monitoring-based service assurance.

   The distinction investigated by IRBC is narrower and concerns the
   object and responsibility of the declaration.  An interconnection
   intent can express a request, expectation, or desired service outcome
   involving capabilities or resources offered by another provider.  An
   IRBC instead concerns a statement made by an AS about routing
   behavior for which that AS takes responsibility.  The distinction is
   not absolute: an interconnection intent may include provider-side
   behavior expectations, and an IBN assurance system may assess those
   expectations.  The question is whether a reusable AS-attributable
   declaration, specifically scoped to BGP behavior and designed for
   assessment by an external domain, provides semantics that are not
   adequately captured by the existing interconnection-intent model and
   its assurance workflow.

   An interconnection intent and an IRBC may be related.  An
   interconnection intent may lead to an operator configuring behavior
   and issuing one or more IRBCs; an IRBC may provide a declaration that
   an interconnection-intent assurance process can consume.  Conversely,
   an IRBC may exist without a preceding bilateral interconnection-
   intent exchange.  The two concepts SHOULD NOT be assumed to be
   separate protocol objects until this relationship has been evaluated
   through concrete scenarios.

B.2.  BGP Policy and Policy Representation

   BGP policy controls route import, selection, and export [RFC4271].
   Operational guidance describes common BGP security and policy
   practices [RFC7454].  RPSL provides a language for describing routing
   policies [RFC2622]; RPSLng extends this approach to IPv6 routing
   policy [RFC4012]; and the Routing Policy YANG Data Model provides a
   vendor-neutral model for configuring and managing routing policy
   [RFC9067].  BGP Communities [RFC1997], Extended Communities
   [RFC4360], and Large Communities [RFC8092] can carry operator-defined
   signaling information.  [RFC8212] defines default eBGP route
   propagation behavior when import or export policy is absent.

   These mechanisms demonstrate that routing policy can be configured,
   represented, and signaled.  IRBC MUST NOT claim that routing policy
   cannot be expressed or that BGP has no means to carry policy-related
   information.  The question is whether a policy representation or
   signal, by itself, also identifies an authorized issuer, states an
   externally understandable commitment for a defined scope, and
   provides semantics for assessing the commitment using evidence
   obtained from another domain.  These properties depend on the
   applicable policy model, trust relationships, and evidence
   mechanisms; they are not automatically provided by the existence of a
   policy language or community value.

B.3.  Routing-Security Mechanisms

   RPKI Route Origin Authorizations (ROAs) defined in [RFC9582] provide
   digitally signed authorization for an AS to originate routes to
   specified prefixes.  BGP Prefix Origin Validation defined in
   [RFC6811] defines how a BGP speaker determines the origin-validation
   state of a route.  Operational guidance for applying origin
   validation on eBGP export is provided by [RFC8893].  ASPA provides
   signed provider-authorization information, and ASPA-based AS_PATH
   verification is specified in current SIDROPS work
   [I-D.ietf-sidrops-aspa-profile] [I-D.ietf-sidrops-aspa-verification].
   BGPsec defined in [RFC8205] provides cryptographic protection for
   AS_PATH propagation information in BGP UPDATE messages .

   IRBC is not a replacement for ROA/ROV, ASPA, BGPsec, BGP Roles, or
   OTC.  Any proposed routing-behavior semantics MUST be shown to add
   value beyond the specific authorization and propagation properties
   already addressed by those mechanisms and MUST coexist with them.

   BGP Roles and the Only-to-Customer (OTC) defined in [RFC9234]
   attribute define role negotiation and specific route-leak prevention
   and detection behavior.  The working definition and classification of
   BGP route leaks are described in [RFC7908].  [RFC9234] defines a
   finite set of BGP Roles for eBGP sessions and corresponding route-
   propagation rules.  It also identifies complex peering relationships,
   for which BGP Roles are not configured on a single eBGP session, and
   notes that per-prefix policies may be used without in-band measures
   to check the correctness of the per-prefix peering configuration.
   These details are relevant to scope-dependent routing behavior, but
   IRBC MUST NOT redefine BGP Role or OTC semantics.
